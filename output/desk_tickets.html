1 · How the agents performed

Ticket 101 · Bulldog tee, size S Observed

What worked

  • Right team, nobody extra. The Boss pulled in Inventory, Accounting, and Customer Service, and skipped Facilities.
  • Accounting linked the bill to the order. get_invoice(501) returned "Rush reprint CC-TEE-WHITE S", 3 days overdue. Accounting tied it to this exact tee and filed invoice_payment #1 for $840, with the amount taken from the invoice row. It filed no second purchase order, since the reprint already exists.
  • Inventory was thorough. Two check_stock calls (size S, then all sizes) and list_vendors found S at 0, M 5 / L 3 / XL 2 in stock, Bulldog Print Co as vendor, and an earliest restock of 2026-09-05.
  • The customer draft was honest and had a date. Customer Service ran last, in its own Boss turn, so the draft says "out of stock … earliest restock estimate September 5" and makes no promise.
  • Cash moved only on approval: −$840 when you approved #1 (Cash tab ledger: $1,000 → $160).

What could improve

  • The alternative sizes never reached Tauhid. Inventory reported M/L/XL in stock, but the Boss's hand-off #3 to Customer Service didn't mention them, so the message offers no alternative. The plan expected one.
  • The draft hints at internal money matters: "awaiting manager approval related to the rush reprint." The date alone would have been enough.
  • "Resolved" isn't "customer served." The ticket resolved while size S is still at 0, and nothing in the app tracks the reprint arriving or tells Tauhid when it does. My view This definition doesn't match how I'd judge the ticket. From the shop's side, 101 isn't done until Tauhid has a tee. I'd split team finished from customer served (see "My perspective" below and §5 B).
  • Minor: the Boss called get_cash_position twice.

Why it matters: a delegated agent only knows what the task text tells it. The fix is either a structured hand-off (the Boss passes Inventory's full report) or letting Customer Service call check_stock itself, which it is allowed to do but didn't.

Ticket 102 · Rent due Observed

What worked

  • Verify, then pay. Facilities' get_lease(1) confirmed the landlord (Elm City Properties), $2,400, and due 2026-09-02 (2 days out). The Boss told Accounting in hand-off #2: "don't file a payment request until rent amount/due date confirmed". Accounting filed rent_payment #3 only on hand-off #3, after Facilities reported.
  • Cash warning across tickets. Accounting and the Boss both stated that rent plus the $840 invoice would leave $160.
  • The leanest run: 3 agents, 13 model calls, 34,822 tokens, about 20 s.
  • The approval did everything it should: −$2,400 (your first approval: $3,400 → $1,000), and the lease's next due date moved to 2026-10-02.

What could improve

  • Accounting was called twice and repeated its work. It made get_lease ×2, get_cash_position ×2, and list_approval_requests ×2, because its first call ran in parallel with Facilities and was told to wait.
  • The scam check passed, but because the notice was genuine, the "possible scam" path was never exercised. We haven't seen it work in a run.

Why it matters: sending tasks in parallel saves time only when they don't depend on each other. Accounting depended on Facilities' answer, so a sequential hand-off (or the planned Facilities → Accounting) would have been cheaper.

Ticket 103 · Bulk hoodie discount Observed

What worked

  • Pricing stayed within the rules. get_pricing gave $22 cost and $58 list. Accounting quoted $49.30 × 20 = $986 (the 15% cap), well above the floor.
  • Shortage found: check_stock showed 8 on hand, 12 short, and the vendor's lead time is 5 days.
  • The server guardrail fired as designed. Purchase order #5 ($264) was filed with the warning that pending money-out ($3,504) exceeded cash ($3,400). The Boss's risks spelled out the $104 overage and recommended rent → invoice → purchase order.
  • No cost leaked to the customer. The Yale AI Club draft mentions no cost or margin.
  • Cash: $0. You rejected #5, so nothing was paid.

What could improve

  • Accounting filed a purchase order it knew cash couldn't cover alongside rent and the invoice, where the plan said to hold it.
  • Internal pricing was passed to Customer Service. The Boss's hand-off #4 included "cost $22". Customer Service has no get_pricing tool precisely so it never sees costs, and the hand-off undid that, even though the draft didn't repeat it.
  • Related requests got out of sync. You rejected the purchase order at 03:15:12. Then message #4 ("expected earliest restock is September 5") was approved at 03:16:11, and the quote for 20 units at 03:16:20. With 8 on hand and no restock ordered, the shop has now quoted 20 hoodies and told the club to expect a restock that isn't on order. The app treats each request on its own and doesn't flag when a rejection makes related ones out of date. My view Rejecting #5 and then approving #4 and #6 were my deliberate choices, not an agent error. I rejected the order because we couldn't serve it for now, and the message and quote don't commit any cash. Looking back, though, my approvals created a promise the app can't keep track of. The quote and the "September 5" restock only hold if someone re-submits the purchase order later, and nothing will remind anyone to (see "My perspective" below).
  • The most expensive run: 47,446 tokens, 4 hand-offs, Accounting invoked twice.

Why it matters: the money protections worked (warning shown, nothing paid without you). The weak spot is consistency between decisions. A simple fix is to warn on approval when another request on the same ticket was rejected, or to let Customer Service re-draft afterwards.

3 · Where one agent with tools would have been simpler

Costs we actually paid for having five agents Observed

  • Tokens: 95–96% of the 124,341 tokens were input. Each agent call re-sends its prompt, the shared rules, and its conversation, and each hand-off starts a new loop. Output was only 1.4k–2.4k tokens per ticket.
  • Repeated lookups: get_ticket ran 9 times for 3 tickets (3 / 2 / 4), because each agent re-reads the ticket. On 102 and 103, Accounting ran twice and repeated get_lease, get_cash_position, get_pricing, and list_approval_requests.
  • Lost and leaked information at hand-offs: 101's alternative sizes were dropped, and 103's unit cost was passed to Customer Service.
  • Harder to trace: 65 / 56 / 74 audit records per ticket. Explaining one decision means following several agent runs.

Where one agent would clearly have been simpler Hypothetical

  • Ticket 102: one agent with get_ticket, get_lease, get_cash_position, and request_approval could finish in roughly four tool calls. The real run took 13 MCP calls plus 3 hand-offs. (Estimate only: we did not run a single-agent version.)
  • Ticket 101's message: if one context holds both the stock check and the draft, the alternative sizes can't get lost.
  • Ticket 103's purchase order: one agent pricing, checking cash, and drafting would see the cash conflict while writing the message, which might avoid the out-of-sync quote and message.

The tradeoff

One agent would give usWe would lose
Fewer tokens and model calls; no repeated lookupsLeast privilege by role. Today Customer Service cannot call get_pricing or file payments, and Facilities can't file payments. One agent would hold every tool, including money requests.
No information lost between hand-offsIndependent checking. On 102, Facilities verified the email separately from the agent that files the payment. One agent would be checking its own work.
Shorter, easier-to-read audit trailParallel fact-finding (the Boss's same-turn hand-offs) and the dashboard's clear "who did what" view

What stays protected either way: the strongest money controls live in the MCP server and your approvals, not in having several agents. Those controls are server-computed amounts, the below-cost refusal, the cash checks, and the human-only approve_request. A single agent still couldn't move a dollar. So for these three tickets, the five-agent design mostly buys extra layers of protection and clarity, at the token cost shown above. Whether that's worth it is a judgment call we haven't discussed. 5 · Three new problems the current implementation could not solve Hypothetical

A · Actually fulfilling the hoodie order

The need: set aside the 8 hoodies for the Yale AI Club, receive the 12 restocked ones, and record the $986 sale and the club's payment.

Why we can't Observed: no tool changes inventory (M is still 8, S is still 0 after the run). Cash can only go down, because approve_request writes payments out but nothing records money in. The approved $986 quote never becomes revenue.

Needed: tools reserve_stock, receive_shipment / adjust_inventory, record_sale (cash in), and an orders table. A Fulfillment / Receiving agent, or Inventory with write access, plus Accounting handling money coming in.

B · Following up over time

The need: tell Tauhid when the reprint arrives, chase Bulldog Print Co if it's late, and prepare October rent (the lease is now due 2026-10-02).

Why we can't: the shop's date is fixed at 2026-08-31 and nothing advances it. There are no reminders or scheduled checks, agents can't open or link tickets, and a resolved ticket can't be reopened from the dashboard.

Needed: tools create_ticket, schedule_followup, advance_shop_day / list_due_items, and reopen_ticket. An Operations Coordinator agent that watches due dates and deliveries and files follow-up tickets for the Boss.

C · Talking to the outside world when cash is tight

The need: actually send the approved messages, place the order with the vendor, and, with only $160 left, ask Bulldog Print Co for a payment plan or ask the club for a deposit before restocking.

Why we can't: "approved" messages aren't emailed anywhere. There's no vendor ordering, approvals pay only the full amount (no partial payments), and there are no deposits, receivables, or cash forecast.

Needed: a send_email tool behind approval (the course's Gmail/Outlook MCP servers could provide it, but they aren't connected to this app), a vendor-order tool, partial payments in approve_request, and deposits plus a cash forecast. A Communications agent (sends approved drafts, reads new emails into tickets) and a Finance Planner agent (forecasts, payment plans).

My perspective

As the human in the loop, I made the six decisions the agents left to me: approving or rejecting each request, and accepting the policies they relied on. This is my reflection on each, tied to what the run records show. My view

1 · Rejecting purchase order #5 ($264, 12 hoodies)

I rejected it because we couldn't serve it for now. By the time I reached it, I had already approved rent ($2,400) and the overdue invoice ($840), so checking was down to $160, less than the $264 the order needed. Rent keeps the shop open. The invoice is the rush reprint for the tee Tauhid is waiting for. The hoodie restock was the only outflow that could wait.

The agents set the decision up well. The server warned when the order was filed that pending money-out ($3,504) exceeded cash ($3,400), and the Boss's decision recommended rent → invoice → purchase order. But they still filed it instead of holding it, as our plan had expected. I think leaving the final call to a person was right; I'd just rather the agents hadn't filed something they already knew couldn't be paid.

What it changes: ticket 103's $0 cash change in the Cash tab was intended, not an oversight.

2 · Approving message #4 and quote #6 after rejecting #5

I thought this was fine because neither moves cash today. The message tells the Yale AI Club that 8 hoodies are available now, the rest depend on a restock with an earliest estimate of September 5, and any bulk price is still pending. The quote, $49.30 × 20 = $986, is within our 15% policy and well above the $22 cost.

Looking back, my approvals exposed a gap. The quote covers 20 units while only 8 exist, and the restock I had just rejected is what would make the other 12 possible. The "September 5" estimate no longer has an order behind it. The app let me approve these in any order without a warning, and if the club accepts, someone has to re-submit the purchase order once cash recovers. A better design would flag the other requests on a ticket when one is rejected, or ask Customer Service to re-draft.

What it changes: the out-of-sync quote and message were a human decision. The real lesson is that dependencies between approvals aren't tracked.

3 · Were the approved messages actually sent?

I don't know, and that is itself one of the more important findings. In our app, approving a customer message only changes its status to "approved". There's no send step, no email connection, and no delivery record. The dashboard showed both messages as approved, but neither Tauhid nor the Yale AI Club necessarily heard anything.

For a real shop this is a customer-trust risk: a manager could believe a customer was told about a delay when they never were. The fix is a send_email tool behind the same approval step (the course's Gmail/Outlook MCP servers could provide it), with a "sent at" time shown on the dashboard.

What it changes: message delivery remains unproven Still open. This is the gap behind §5 C.

4 · Does "resolved = the team finished" fit?

Not really. The dashboard marks a ticket resolved as soon as the agents finish, and we chose that rule to match the assignment's wording. But measured by what the customer or shop actually got:

  • 101: Tauhid still has no tee. Size S is at 0, and the invoice is paid, but nothing tracks the reprint or tells him when it arrives.
  • 103: the club has a quote, but only 8 of its 20 hoodies exist.
  • 102: this is the only ticket that's truly done: rent paid, lease rolled to 2026-10-02.

I'd separate team finished from customer served / closed and keep a ticket open until the outcome is delivered. The current label hides unfinished work.

What it changes: "3/3 resolved" is accurate for the agents' work, but I'd count only one ticket (102) as fully done.

5 · The 15% bulk-discount cap

I'm comfortable with it. On ticket 103 it produced $49.30 against a $58 list price and $22 cost, still about a 55% margin on the discounted price. The MCP server refuses any quote below cost, so even a bad model answer can't sell at a loss.

It's worth noting where the rule lives: it's written into Accounting's prompt, not stored in the database or enforced by a tool. Changing it means editing a prompt, and the server only enforces the cost floor, not the 15%. In a real shop I'd keep discount policy as data that the request_approval tool checks.

What it changes: the 103 quote stands as within policy, and the policy is accepted rather than assumed.

6 · Ending at $160 in checking

I'm fine with it for this scenario. Rent kept the shop open and the overdue invoice was holding up a customer's order, so both were worth paying even though together they left only $160. That thin balance is also exactly why I rejected the purchase order.

But $160 is a fragile cushion. The next rent is due 2026-10-02, and the app has no way to record money coming in: the approved $986 quote doesn't add cash. Any surprise bill would hit an account that can't cover it. If this were a real shop, I'd want a minimum-balance rule that Accounting checks before filing payments, and a cash forecast that includes upcoming rent.

What it changes: no minimum-balance rule is needed for this exercise, but cash planning is one of the clearest missing capabilities (§5 C).
